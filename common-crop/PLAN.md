# Common Crop: farm-to-neighbour buying club for Nottinghamshire

*Working name. Check Companies House and the IPO trademark search before using it.*
*This is general guidance, not legal advice. Confirm the food-safety points with the Environmental Health team at the council where your hub will be, and the structure with Co-operatives UK or a small-business adviser.*

---

## 1. Short answer

| Question | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **A) Legally plausible?** | **Yes.** Community food hubs and buying groups are common in the UK. Registration is free and fairly light for fruit and veg. Meat takes more care. |
| **B) Workable?** | **Yes, if you keep it small and use collection points.** Home delivery is what sank the well-funded attempts. |
| **C) Unsaturated in Notts?** | **Partly.** Veg-box delivery is crowded (Riverford, Abel & Cole, Oddbox all deliver here). Group bulk buys with local collection, especially meat shares and seasonal gluts, have far fewer players. You need to test demand before spending money. |
| **Can you pay yourself?** | **Yes.** "Community" doesn't mean unpaid. A CIC or co-op can pay a reasonable salary. What it can't do is hand surplus profit to outside shareholders. |

**Recommendation: go, as a 3-month pilot.** One hub, 3–5 farms, 50–100 founding households, fortnightly orders. You only scale up if the pilot numbers work.

## 2. The big question: does buying on the consumers' behalf remove the red tape?

**No, not once it's organised and regular. But the red tape is much lighter than it sounds.**

UK food law (Food Safety Act 1990, retained General Food Law Regulation 178/2002, Food Safety and Hygiene (England) Regulations 2013) looks at **what you actually do**, not who owns the food or who paid for it. A "food business" is any undertaking, **profit-making or not**, that carries out any stage of producing, processing or **distributing** food. The FSA's test is whether there's *"a certain continuity of activities and a certain degree of organisation"*.

- **Five friends splitting a sack of potatoes now and then:** not a food business. No registration needed.
- **A website, an ordering cycle, a hub, open to the public:** that's a food business, even if every penny belongs to the customers and you're just the agent.

The agent model doesn't get you out of registration. What it does do is **keep you in the lightest category**:

- You're a **distributor**, not a processor. The farm, abattoir or cutting plant carries the heavy compliance (approvals, health marks, labelling).
- **The golden rule: never open, cut, repack or relabel anything.** Everything goes in and out exactly as the producer packed it. Once you start portioning meat or bagging loose produce, you pick up extra duties, and with meat you may need approval.

### What you actually have to do

| Requirement | Effort | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Register as a food business** with the council where the hub is (Nottingham City, Rushcliffe, Gedling, Broxtowe, etc.) | Free, online form, **28 days before you start** | You'll get a Food Hygiene Rating inspection eventually. Aim for 5. |
| **Food safety management system** | A weekend | Use the FSA's free *Safer Food, Better Business* pack (retail version). |
| **Level 2 Food Hygiene** certificate for whoever runs the hub | ~£15–30 online, half a day | Also do it for volunteers who handle chilled goods. |
| **Cold chain** for meat, dairy and eggs | Main cost | Chilled food at 8 °C or below by law, and you should aim for 0–5 °C. Probe thermometer plus a temperature log each collection day. Frozen boxes (−18 °C) are simpler. |
| **Traceability** | Easy with software | Record which farm supplied what and who received it ("one step back, one step forward"). The website database does this automatically. |
| **Allergen information** | Low for fresh produce | Pre-packed items already carry producer labels. Keep allergen info available for anything else. |
| **Public & product liability insurance** | ~£300–800/yr | Add employer's liability if you hire anyone, and business-use cover for any vehicle. |
| **ICO data protection fee** | ~£40–60/yr | You're holding customer names, addresses and payment records. |
| **Consumer law** | Terms and conditions page | Consumer Rights Act 2015 and Consumer Contracts Regulations. Perishables are exempt from the 14-day cancellation right, but you must show who you are, prices, and collection terms before payment. |

### Product-specific traps

- **Meat:** must come from an **approved** abattoir or cutting plant, vacuum-packed, with the oval **health mark** and a use-by date. Buy boxes already butchered and labelled (many Notts livestock farms sell this way). Don't split carcasses yourself.
- **Eggs:** ungraded eggs from small flocks can only be sold **direct by the producer** (farm gate, door-to-door, local public market). Through a hub they must be **graded and stamped** by a registered packing centre. Pick egg suppliers who already do this.
- **Raw (unpasteurised) milk:** in England it can only be sold **by the producing farm directly**, so a hub can't resell it. Pasteurised milk and cheese are fine.
- **Fruit and veg:** lowest risk. Whole, unwashed produce is the easiest thing to start with.

> I read "basic farm amenities" as farm staples (eggs, milk, flour, honey, oil, maybe firewood). Non-food items like firewood carry their own rules (e.g. "Ready to Burn" certification for small volumes of wood fuel).

## 3. Money: collecting cash upfront without walking into financial regulation

The "shoppers put money in first, the pooled money buys in bulk" idea is sound, but **the wording and structure matter.**

- **Never call it "investing".** If people hand over money expecting a financial return, you're in FCA territory (collective investment schemes, financial promotions). Call it a **pre-order** or **pledge**: money for specific goods at a stated price.
- **Crowd-buy with a threshold** (the site is built around this): a half beef is split into 8 shares, and the order only goes to the farm once 8 are pledged. If the target isn't hit by the close date, **nobody is charged** (or everyone is refunded in full).
- **Technically:** card authorisations expire after about 7 days, so either keep order windows to 7 days or less, or save the card (Stripe SetupIntent) and charge only when the target is hit. Keep customer money in a **separate bank account** from running costs.
- **Pay farmers in 7 days or less.** This is your biggest bargaining chip (see §6).
- If the business failed while holding pre-payments, customers would be unsecured creditors. Short cycles and a small float keep that risk tiny.

**Later, for real capital** (a cold room, a van): a **community share offer**. People buy withdrawable shares in a Community Benefit Society. It's legal, exempt from most FCA prospectus rules, and made for exactly this kind of project. Get it set up with Co-operatives UK / the Community Shares Unit.

## 4. Legal structure: which one?

| Option | Cost / time | Salary for you? | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | Free, same day | Yes (it's your profit) | A 4-week test only. You're personally liable. |
| **CIC limited by guarantee** ✅ | ~£65 at Companies House, 1–3 weeks | **Yes, reasonable salary** | **Recommended start.** Limited liability, "community" is written into law (asset lock), eligible for many grants, quick to set up. |
| Co-operative society (consumer or multi-stakeholder) | ~£100–400 via Co-operatives UK model rules, 4–8 weeks | Yes | If you want members (shoppers + farmers + workers) to formally own it. |
| Community Benefit Society | As above | Yes | Needed for a community share offer later. |
| Charity | Slow, restrictive | Hard | Not a fit for trading. |

**Plan:** start as a **CIC limited by guarantee** with a small board (you + 2 others, ideally one farmer and one customer). If a community share raise makes sense in year 2, convert or set up a society then.

**Paying yourself:** set a modest salary or hourly rate and record it in board minutes. Put surplus back into lower prices, equipment and the community. That's exactly what CICs are designed for.

**Tax:** register with HMRC. Most food is **zero-rated for VAT**. The VAT registration threshold is £90k turnover, which you'll pass at around 250 active households (see §5). Because most sales are zero-rated, registering often *helps*, since you can reclaim VAT on equipment. Get an accountant once you're live.

## 5. Business model

### Recommended: pre-order buying club with collection hubs and crowd-buys

```
 Order window (Fri→Thu 8pm)   Aggregate & order   Farms deliver   Members collect
 ────────────────────────────▶ ───────────────────▶ ───────────────▶ ──────────────
 members pledge / pre-pay      one PO per farm      one drop per hub  Sat 10am–1pm
 crowd-buys fill to target     only if target met   (farm or our run)  at hub
```

1. **Fortnightly cycle** (weekly once busy): orders open Friday, close Thursday 8pm, collection Saturday morning.
2. **Two product types:**
   - **Standard lines**: eggs, veg bags, potatoes, apples. Always on.
   - **Crowd-buys**: big bulk items that only go ahead when enough shares are pledged (half a beef, a pig, a lamb, a tonne of Bramleys, a pallet of flour).
3. **Collection hubs**, not home delivery. A community centre, church hall, café or school for 3 hours. This is the main cost advantage. Paid home delivery can come later.
4. **Transport:** farms drop to the hub as part of their own run (build that into the price), or one volunteer or paid "farm loop" run in a hired van for farms that can't deliver.

### How it makes money: a transparent margin

Add **~15% to the farm-gate price** and show customers exactly where it goes. Example for every £10 spent:

| Farmer | Card fees | Hub costs (hall, insurance, cold chain) | Coordinator wage | Reserve |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| £8.70 | £0.20 | £0.40 | £0.55 | £0.15 |

Optional later: **£3–4/month membership** that drops the margin to ~8%, which gives steady income that doesn't depend on basket size.

### Can it cover a salary? (illustrative numbers)

Assumptions: £35 average order per active household per cycle, 26 cycles/year, 13% net margin after card fees.

| Active households | Turnover / yr | Margin | Overheads | Left for wages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | £91,000 | £11,800 | ~£4,000 | **~£7,800** (paid side-project) |
| 250 | £227,500 | £29,600 | ~£5,500 | **~£24,000** (one modest salary) |
| 500 | £455,000 | £59,200 | ~£9,000 | **~£50,000** (two part-time roles) |

Meat crowd-buys raise the average basket a lot (a beef share is ~£125), so these figures are conservative on basket size and optimistic on how often people order. **The pilot is there to replace these assumptions with real numbers.**

### Be honest with customers about price

Supermarkets are very efficient on basic veg, so you won't always beat them on loose carrots. The wins are:

- **Meat bought as shares**: often well below butcher and supermarket prices for equivalent quality.
- **Seasonal gluts and grade-outs** ("wonky" or oversize), which farmers otherwise sell cheaply or waste.
- **Freshness and traceability**: picked this week, 14 miles away, from a named farm.
- **Eggs, apples, potatoes by the sack**: bulk units supermarkets don't sell.

Sell it as **"better food, fairer to farmers, and often cheaper"**, not "always cheapest".

### Lessons from people who tried

- **The Food Assembly UK** (closed 2018) and **Farmdrop** (closed early 2020s): venture-funded, delivery-heavy, and grew faster than their margins. Home delivery and marketing costs ate them.
- **Growing Communities** (Hackney) and the many **Open Food Network** hubs: small, collection-based, community-owned, still going after years. **Copy these.**

## 6. Getting the best bulk deals from local suppliers

Farmers usually get squeezed on price, payment terms and waste. Offer them the opposite and they'll give you better prices:

1. **Pay fast:** on delivery, or within 7 days (supermarket terms are often 30–60+).
2. **Predictable volume:** a standing order every fortnight they can plan around, and eventually committing to part of a crop before planting.
3. **Take the whole crop spec:** accept oversize, undersize and cosmetic grade-outs.
4. **Buy the glut:** when Bramleys or courgettes all ripen at once, run a crowd-buy that week.
5. **Price fairly and openly:** offer **farm-gate price + 10–20%** (more than wholesale gives them, less than they'd get at retail) and keep your own margin transparent.
6. **Make delivery easy:** one drop point, one time, one invoice per cycle.
7. **Marketing for them:** farm profile pages, photos, "meet the farmer" days at the hub.

**Where to find farms in Notts:**
- Stallholders at local farmers' markets (they already sell direct and are set up for it).
- Farm shops and roadside honesty boxes around the Vale of Belvoir, Southwell/Newark, the Trent valley and the Sherwood sandlands (potatoes and carrots). Southwell is where the **Bramley apple** comes from.
- NFU East Midlands, LEAF, the Pasture for Life directory, and local butchers with approved cutting rooms who can supply share boxes.
- Open Food Network UK's producer map.

**Start with 3–5 suppliers**: one veg grower, one orchard, one egg producer (graded), one livestock farm selling boxed meat. Visit each one and agree prices in writing for the pilot.

## 7. Crowdfunding: Kickstarter or something else?

**Use Crowdfunder UK rather than Kickstarter.** Both work, but Crowdfunder is UK-focused, common for community food projects, and often has **match funding** (council, National Lottery and corporate pots) that can double what you raise. Kickstarter's audience is more global and product-focused.

**Target: £5,000–£8,000** (small, reachable, all-or-nothing gives credibility).

| Use of funds | Approx. |
|---|---|
| Cold chain: 2 second-hand commercial fridges or chest freezer, 12 insulated crates, probe thermometers and loggers | £1,800 |
| Insurance (year 1) | £700 |
| Incorporation, ICO, accountant setup | £400 |
| Hygiene training for 6 volunteers | £150 |
| Signage, banners, branding, printed flyers | £700 |
| Website hosting, domain, payment setup | £300 |
| Pilot hub hire (3 months) | £500 |
| Platform and payment fees (~5–8%) | £500 |
| Contingency | £500–1,000 |

**Reward ideas:** founding-member badge and name on the hub wall (£10), a £25 pledge worth £28 of first-order credit (cap how many, since credit is a liability), a founding Bramley sack (£15), a founding beef share (£130), "Adopt-a-hub" for local businesses (£250).

**Don't use crowdfund money as float to buy customers' food.** Customers' own pre-payments pay for that.

## 8. First steps (12 weeks)

**Weeks 1–2: validate**
- [ ] 2-minute survey (Google Form or Tally), shared in local Facebook groups, Nextdoor, school newsletters, allotment societies. Ask what they'd buy, how much, how often, which area, and whether they'd collect. **Goal: 150 responses, 60+ "definitely yes".**
- [ ] Competitor map: veg boxes, farm shops and markets within 10 miles of your chosen hub.
- [ ] Visit 6–8 farms and get indicative farm-gate prices.

**Weeks 3–4: set up**
- [ ] Register the CIC. Open a business bank account (plus a separate pre-order account).
- [ ] Find a hub venue (community centre or church hall, Saturday morning).
- [ ] **Register as a food business with the council (28 days' notice starts now).**
- [ ] Insurance, ICO fee, Level 2 hygiene, Safer Food Better Business pack.

**Weeks 5–6: build**
- [ ] Put this website live (static, free hosting) with a founding-member signup.
- [ ] For actual ordering, **start on Open Food Network UK** (open-source, built for food hubs) rather than building checkout yourself. Build custom features (crowd-buys) only once the pilot proves them.
- [ ] Launch the Crowdfunder with farm photos and a short video.

**Weeks 7–12: pilot**
- [ ] 3 fortnightly cycles, then weekly if demand is there.
- [ ] Track per cycle: households ordering, average basket, margin, hours worked, waste, complaints, temperature logs.
- [ ] **Go/no-go at week 12:** is repeat ordering ≥60%, is the margin covering costs, and are the hours sustainable? If yes, add a second hub. If no, you've spent little and learned a lot.

## 10. The pot model (v2)

This replaces the simple crowd-buys in §5 with local pots, earmarked credit and QR pickup.

### How it works

1. **Join a pot.** Your postcode puts you in the nearest **pot**: a local group of up to 120 households with one hub, one collection slot and one group chat. When a pot fills, a new one opens at the same hub on a new slot (e.g. Sneinton Pot 1 at 10–11:30, Pot 2 at 11:30–1).
2. **Top up and earmark.** Members add credit, then put money on the items they want and set **"most I'd use"** (e.g. £10 on strawberries, max 3 punnets).
3. **All pots buy together.** Pots are social and logistical groups, but every pot's money is combined for buying. Bulk tiers are set per item (e.g. strawberries £2.40 → £1.95 at 160 punnets → £1.60 at 480 → £1.35 at 1,200), so each new member lowers the price for everyone.
4. **Close and allocate (Thursday 8pm).** The engine (`site/engine.js`) works out, for each item:
   - **Which tier:** it tries the cheapest tier first. At that price each member wants `min(their max, budget ÷ price)` units. If the total, rounded down to whole cases, reaches the tier's minimum, that tier is bought. Otherwise it tries the next tier up.
   - **Who gets what:** units are shared as evenly as possible up to each member's max ("water-filling"). Any odd units left after rounding to whole cases go in the order people signed up.
   - **Carry-over:** whatever a member earmarked but didn't need **stays on that item** for next week, so they top up less next time. They can also move it to another item, or to unassigned credit and withdraw it.
5. **Pick up with a QR code.** Each member has a code per cycle. Scanning it at the hub hands over their parcel and completes the purchase. Credit moves from "bought, awaiting pickup" to "spent".
6. **Collecting for someone else.** A member can nominate someone in their pot. It only takes effect when that person accepts. After that, **only the nominee's code releases the parcel**, and scanning it releases both.
7. **Not collected:** food is held until 4pm where possible, then donated. It isn't refunded.
8. **Delivery:** optional, for a flat fee per cycle, where a pot has it.

**Your strawberry example:** 430 people put money on strawberries, but most families can't get through a whole flat. Each person caps it at 1–4 punnets. The network buys 127 flats at £1.60 because it's 168 punnets short of the £1.35 tier. Everyone gets what they asked for (up to their cap), and the rest of their money stays on strawberries for next week. The demo shows exactly this.

### The "concrete method" for money

- **Append-only ledger.** Every penny is an event: deposit, move, commit, collect, forfeit, or withdraw. Balances are **calculated** from the list, never typed in. History can't be edited; corrections are new events. The database enforces this (`backend/schema.sql`).
- **Per-item credit.** Each member's credit sits in buckets: unassigned, one per item, "bought, awaiting pickup", spent, forfeited and withdrawn. The Credit tab shows all of these.
- **Guards:** you can't overspend a bucket, and you can't withdraw earmarked money until you move it back. A parcel can't be collected twice, and a delegated parcel won't release to the original member's code. The test suite covers each of these (`tests/engine.test.js`).
- **Deterministic:** the same inputs always give the same allocation. Store the engine version with each cycle's results so any cycle can be re-run and audited.

### What these features change legally

| Feature | Issue | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Holding credit balances** | Money held for later use and withdrawable on request can start to look like e-money or deposit-taking, which are FCA-regulated. A prepayment to one business for its own goods is generally outside those regimes. | Credit can only be spent on Common Crop food. Withdrawals go **back to the original card** as a refund, never to any bank account. Cap balances (e.g. £250), and prompt or refund credit unused for 3 months. Keep member money in a separate account (or as Stripe customer balance). **Get a one-off opinion from a payments solicitor before launch.** This is the biggest legal unknown in v2. |
| **"Pot" wording** | A pooled fund with members sharing outcomes can look like a collective investment scheme. | The pot is only a **pricing mechanism**. Legally, each member pre-pays Common Crop for their own food, and Common Crop sells to them. Never use "invest", "fund" or "returns". |
| **Tier pricing** | Consumers must know the total price before they're bound. | The member's budget is the **most they can pay**, and the price can only fall from what's shown. Say "you pay at most £X, usually less". The final price is fixed at close. |
| **Supermarket savings** | Price comparisons must be accurate, like-for-like and verifiable. Since the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, the CMA can fine directly. | Compare the same weight, grade and production standard. Date each check and name the retailer. Keep evidence (the `reference_prices` table). If in doubt, drop the claim. |
| **No refund if not collected** | The term must be fair and prominent (Consumer Rights Act 2015). You can't exclude liability for faulty food or negligence. | Short plain rules, agreed at signup and shown again at checkout. Send a reminder on the day, keep a clear window, donate rather than bin. The Rules tab has draft wording. |
| **Someone else collecting** | When does it stop being your responsibility? | Under the Consumer Rights Act, **risk passes when goods reach the consumer *or a person they've identified*** to take them. A mutually accepted nomination plus the scan record is exactly that. You still answer for the food being safe and as described when it left you. |
| **QR codes** | A copied screenshot could be used to take someone's food. | Codes are signed on the server, change every cycle, and work once. The volunteer sees the member's first name and parcel list. Rules: treat your code like a ticket. |
| **Group chat** | A chat between users is a "user-to-user service" under the **Online Safety Act 2023**: illegal-content risk assessment, a reporting route, terms of use, handling complaints. | **Pilot with a WhatsApp Community per pot** (you're a group admin, not the service provider), then build in-app chat once it proves useful. Members must be 18+. The app already has an opt-in, and the schema has report and remove support. |
| **Delivery** | Food transport hygiene and insurance. | Chilled boxes, a temperature check at dispatch, business-use vehicle insurance. If you use couriers, have a written contract. |
| **Donating uncollected food** | Donated food still has to be safe. | Partner with a food bank or community fridge (FareShare-style rules). Only donate before the use-by date, and log it. |

### Operations sizing

- **120 households per pot, one 90-minute slot:** about 80 pickups an hour, which two volunteers can handle with a scanner and pre-packed labelled bags.
- **Pre-pack by pot:** the engine's allocation becomes a packing list per member. Bag and label everything on Friday night or Saturday morning.
